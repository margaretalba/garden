### What is digital gardening?
Digital gardening is like a personal wiki, a place where you can not only collect different thoughts and ideas, but connect them together. Similar to a common-place journal or a second-brain. It’s ultimately a place on the internet (or at least your computer) that encourages continuous learning and curiosity that's specific to you.

Like have you gone down a wikipedia hole? You maybe watched a movie and it made you look up the actor, and maybe who they dated, and before you know it you land on an article about the [sweater curse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweater_curse). 

Now imagine that, but it's articles of things *you're* interested in or media that *you* consumed. Maybe you wrote about the concepts of said movie. And you realize you read an article about that same concept earlier that year, that was mentioned the sweater curse. You can connect that movie to the sweater curse with things that you were interested in. And then maybe that  generates new insight and potentially a related, but completely new concept. (Also, the sweater curse is my favorite wikipedia article, it's a fun read.)

Maggie Appleton, a research engineer with a robust digital garden herself, wrote that a typical digital garden consists of the following things:

1. **Topography over Timelines:** Digital gardens are not considered a blogs, because it's non-chronological. Instead the content is structured through hyperlinks by connecting two different ideas or sources to come up with a uniquely you idea. 
2. **Continuous Growth:** Digital gardens are meant to be grown over time, always evolving and changing. 
3. **Imperfection & Learning:** Your thinking and creative process is public, and that's okay! In fact, it's part of the ethos of a digital garden. 
4. **Playful, Personal, and Experimental:** There isn't necessarily a theme or template to a digital garden. Unless you count the theme and template as you. Are you interested in knitted socks and the history of cars? Do you like long-form essays or short notes? You can build your digital garden to match the way you think and create.
5. **Intercropping & Content Diversity:** We get our information from a myriad of places, and you can root them all in a digital garden. It's the place where you can plant a tidbit from a podcast, a photo from your camera roll, an article from a book, a link to a youtube video. This is where it all can live.
6. **Independent Ownership:** Ideally, digital gardens are independently owned, not hosted on a platform like Instagram or Pinterest where you are confined by the rules and algorithm of the app. 

And if you put it on the internet, it's learning in public. You're allowing people to see your references, inspiration, notes, and overall creative process.
### How did it start?
The first recorded mention of the term [Digital Gardening is Mark Bernstein's 1998 essay ](https://faculty.washington.edu/farkas/HCDE510-Fall2012/BernsteinGardens.pdf)Hypertext Gardens. You can actually still read this essay online now, though when I first started researching for this video, the website was live and not it's not. I was able to access it via the Wayback machine though. It's very old internet in a nostalgic way.

In his essay, he likens different spaces of the internet as either wilderness or a corporate office. Think of it as unorganized text document with all your notes is wilderness, and let's say an app, like Instagram is the corporate office. One is almost unusable, depending on how large it is and the other is rigid - not really allowing creativity in it's structure.

"Gardens and parks lie between farmland and wilderness. The garden is farmland that delights the senses, designed for delight rather than commodity. The park is wilderness, tamed for our enjoyment. Since most hypertext aims neither for the wilderness of unplanned content, nor for the straight rows of formal organization, gardens and parks can inspire a new approach to hypertext design and can help us understand the patterns we observe in fine hypertext writing"

Bernstein's digital garden has cookie recipes and notes on every book he's read. 

How we use digital gardens today is more similar to Mike Caufield's 2015 essay. He goes deeper into the gardening metaphor by describing the internet we know today as streams. In his essay he says:

"In the stream metaphor you don’t experience the Stream by walking around it and looking at it, or following it to its end. You jump in and let it flow past. You feel the force of it hit you as things float by.

It’s not that you are passive in the Stream. You can be active. But your actions in there — your blog posts, @ mentions, forum comments — exist in a context that is collapsed down to a simple timeline of events that together form a narrative."

This is honestly how I felt when I was a creative marketing myself on Instagram. Though in the moment, it felt more like a hamster wheel than a stream. Every photo I posted felt like it had the "lifespan" of maybe MAYBE three days. It would be at the top of the feed and then by the end of the day, washed away with the rest of the content that was being created. And then the cycle goes. 

In contrast, imagine a digital garden. You plant ideas as seeds, and continue to water them by adding to it and connecting them all together. As Mike writes, It's a tool to think with, not a tool to publish with.
### How digital gardening helps your creativity

Okay, now you know what digital gardening is, and it's origin story. But why should you start one and how does it help your creativity?

1. **You have a home for all the things you're inspired by**
   And I mean ALLL the things. It can be a book you really liked and a movie that you loved, an article you read. It can be images, a quote, a screenshot. All of these are possible items you can include in your digital garden. 
   
   And you can take it one step further. Anna Howard in her video essay about digital gardens talks about using a digital garden to consume media thoughtfully. After you watch said movie, write what your takeaways were, what did you notice? Did you have any unanswered questions? Which leads me to...
2. **You become an active learner** 
   Since digital gardening, I've been actually taking more notes. After I watch a How To video, I take notes. A library book, notes. This helps the information sink in, and gives them staying power. In the past, after I watch how-to video, I would just watch the next how-to video that followed. Whatever I learned simply dissolved with time. With note-taking, you're actively learning and staying curious. And isn't curiosity a huge part of creativity?
3. **You know yourself better**
   In Keep Going by Austin Kleon, he writes about how to stay creative. One of his points is to *Pay attention to what you pay attention to*. What are the things that you gravitate towards? That bring you joy? Then he recommends collecting these things as a whole and look for patterns.
   
   Digital gardening does a great job of mapping out your interests in a way that makes it easier to spot patterns. It's even easier when you use the tag feature. More on that later.
   
   And through these patterns, it reveals something about you to you. What concept keeps coming up? For example, when assembling my digital garden, I noticed I gravitate towards little metal boxes: pill boxes, vintage cigarette cases, etc. Now I want to pull on that thread, and create my own. Seeing your interests as a whole can spark an idea.
   
   Like what Kleon says in his book, "When you have a system for going back through your work, you can better see the bigger picture of what you've been up to and what you should do next." 
4. **It's a way to be online without being ONLINE.** 
   There's less pressure and there's no algorithm. A digital garden is not like a blog or Instagram - you don't have to publish an article or even finished thoughts. You don't even have to publish it! Digital gardening is not focused on an outcome. It's driven by curiosity, and it's a journey. 
   
   I also think apps are noisy. Between the ads, and the short form videos, and the literal endless feed of mostly pointless information- it's hard to stop and actually listen to your own thoughts. Like was that my idea? Or just an regurgitated idea from a regurgitated idea from a regurgitated idea? Is my idea just the wine bucket of whatever is trending on socials today? think I underestimate the influence of a dozen 15 second videos saying the same thing. But this may be a just-me thing, so let me know your own thoughts! 
### How to use it as a creative

Okay, now how can *you* use it. Something I struggled with, and why it took me so long to adopt digital gardening was that I didn't find many public digital gardens that I could relate to.

Because of it's non-linear format and interconnecting structure, the people who took to digital gardening best were people who could code, developers, etc. I considered myself a coder until I had to open a github account and look up what clone a repository meant in order to publish my digital garden. More on that later.

Even at the time of this video, I haven't found many public digital gardens that feature makers, creatives. Even though I think they would benefit so much from a digital garden. I did find a few and I'll make a separate substack about that.

It wasn't until I came across [Carolyn Yoo's digital garden](https://garden.carolynyoo.com/) that it finally clicked with me. Carolyn is an artist and writer and I love her [substack](https://cyoo.substack.com/). Her digital garden is public and it's a personal wiki: A list of how-tos. A collection of concepts. References. Honestly it was seeing the list of how-tos that inspired me to start my own. I consume so many how-to videos/articles - it's so nice to compile them in a space I can visit and navigate easily.

**A few things you can plant in your digital garden include:**
Tutorials on things you're interested in, but havent tried yet
Articles on business best practices
Moodboard
Reviews
Book reports
Mini-essays (or full essays)
Recipes
Story concepts
Half-formed ideas


**If you're an artist,** you can use a digital garden to collect design inspiration from books and online. You can document your creative process, and if you're feeling up for it, show it to potential employers in an interview. What I've been doing, is going through my different art books and taking photos of things that inspire me and putting them in my digital garden - so it's all in one place where I can see.

**If you're a writer,** you can collect quotes, and write stories. Actually many writers use Obsidian to create their literary worlds. You can expand on intriguing concepts and topics or collect conversations and passages.

**If you're a maker,** you can collect how-to tutorials that you find on YouTube or blogs on your craft.

It's also a great tool to write a project diary. If you're working on a specific project like I am for this signet idea I have, you can write down your findings, what worked, what didn't, what adjustments you made. I find collecting these help your future self as well in case you want to ever reiterate on that project. You don't have to think, what did I buy for that project? Or for me, where did I get this casted? Project diaries are very useful.
### How to Start Your Own
If you're a coder - you can build your own! If not, there's a couple tools you can use: [Obsidian](https://obsidian.md/), [Notion](https://www.notion.com/) are just two of them. There's also [Are.na](https://www.are.na/) , [Roam Garden](https://roam.garden/), and [Sublime](https://sublime.app/). In this video, I'll just talk about the first two, since they are free and you can make unlimited pages.

Whichever you choose largely depends on your needs. I like Obsidian because of it's simplicity. It's a free app that's mostly text-heavy, but they do have this feature called canvas where you can map out photos. I use that to collect inspo pics.

If you want to publish your Obsidian digital garden, it is $8 a month. If you want to publish online for free like I have you can use [Quartz](https://quartz.jzhao.xyz/), but you'll need a little github experience, or at the very least an account. My favorite little feature is the brain map. It's just sooo satisfying to see it fill up the more you write and add to your digital garden.

Notion is great too! It's also free and doesn't require any coding knowledge. There are also digital gardening templates that you can start with so you don't have to build completely from scratch. 

What I've personally found is sometimes Notion offers me too much personalization and freedom, that I get caught up in making it look good rather than actually writing. But again! It's all a preference. 

### Conclusion

Since digital gardening, I'm actually motivated to collect sources and write my thoughts on them. It's made me not only consume content thoughtfully, but actually seek out and research. I want a loooong list of How tos for future me to visit. I want to see the patterns develop in the little brain map. But those are things that come with time and tending to, just like an actual garden.

Right now, I'm working a flipping wax signet and it's been so cool to use the Obisidan canvas feature to paste inspo pics and map them all together. I've also been journaling through my creative process with this project which you can read in my digital garden at [garden.margaretalba.com](https://garden.margaretalba.com/). You'll find that most of my digital garden is about digital gardening. But what can I tell you!! It's my current hyperfixation!



### Conclusion

My current hyperfixation as of late? Digital gardening.

Since deleting Instagram and Tiktok from my phone, I've been struggling on how to show up on the internet. It's ironic because I've always been able to find a community way before Instagram and Facebook entered the picture. I've been a part of forums, built my own website, started Neopets guilds. I would make flashing GIFs (called blinkies) and PSDs (cropped out image of celebrities) for visitors to my website. 

Back then, the internet felt *fun* with boundless opportunities to be creative. 

I first heard about Digital Gardening back in 2020. And at the time, it didn't make sense. It's not a blog? It's not a Pinterest board? What is it? I struggled to apply it to my own life and needs. 

I then watched an incredible video essay by Anna Howard about digital gardening. And I was intrigued! She did a great job articulating not only what a digital garden is, but how it could benefit your creative life.

I downloaded Obsidian, even wrote a few notes here and there. But I had this pervasive feeling that I wasn't using it to its full potential and that it wasnt actually solving any problem I had.

Then enter [Carolyn Yoo's substack on Digital Gardening](https://cyoo.substack.com/p/intro-to-digital-gardening-a-quietly). Seeing her actual digital garden really made me see the potential of having one as a maker. A little wikipedia database personalized to your hyperfixations, obssessions, or deep dives. 

I've only been using a digital garden for a week and already I see my relationship with the internet transforming into something positive. For example, I'm collecting photos from the Public Domain and Internet Archive for project ideas. I'm re-listening to some of my favorite YouTube essays so I can log my favorite takeaways.

I am SO excited to grow this digital garden. 

So if you consider yourself a creative and want to make a digital garden, read on.

For context, I grew up with the internet - I've had a xanga, blogspots, my own website, multiple neopets accounts, active on forums since I was maybe 9 years old. It's almost come to the point where not posting something feels unnatural to me. And this is not to say, I miss the views/likes/comments. I don't mind creating something and not sharing it. To be honest, I haven't told any of my friends I have this video channel. 

All this to say, sharing is part of the creative process for me. So how do I do that without the usual channels: instagram, tiktok,

But not like read a big text book kid of learning, at least not yet. I gravitate towards a substack, or a youtube essay, or a book. I go into deep rabbit holes for weeks. And what do I show for it? There is something to acting out on a creative impulse immediately. But what about the random hyperfixations and rabbit-holes?  The ideas that spark curiosity or joy within you, but you don't know what to do with just yet?

Sure there is Pinterest. But at least for me images get forgotten or lost until you actually open up the folder later. I can't tell you how many times Pinterest tells me I've already pinned an idea. Like, what do you mean I've already seen this before?
