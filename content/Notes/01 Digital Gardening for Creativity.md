### What is digital gardening?
Digital gardening is like a personal wiki, a place where you can not only collect different thoughts and ideas, but connect them together. Similar to a common-place journal, you can also call it a second-brain. It’s ultimately a place on the internet (or at least your computer) that encourages continuous learning and curiosity that's specific to you.

Like have you gone down a wikipedia hole? You maybe watched a movie and it made you look up the actor, and maybe who they dated, and it turns out they are related to this university, that was related to a scandal you heard about in another way? 

Now imagine that, but it's articles of things *you're* interested in or media that you consumed. Maybe you wrote about the concepts of said movie. And you realize you read an article about that same concept earlier that year. Which maybe referenced a YouTube video that goes deeper in the subject. Which then generates new insight and potentially a related, but completely new concept.

In the [[Digital gardening with a forest atlas]] it says "The gardening approach is used, for example, to conceptualize online spaces where seeds (or thoughts) are nurtured and grown, crossfertilization of formerly separate entries allow for new reflections, and gardening processes require constant care to flourish" 

It's not considered a blog, because it's non-chronological. You're encouraged to build upon topics over time, Connect two different ideas or sources, and come up with a uniquely you idea. 

And if you put it on the internet, it's learning in public. You're allowing people to see your references, inspiration, notes, and overall creative process.
### How did it start?
The first recorded mention of the term Digital Gardening is Mark Bernstein's 1998 essay Hypertext Gardens. You can actually still read this essay online now. It's very old internet in a nostalgic way. But how we use them today is more similar to Mike Caufield's 2015 essay. He goes deeper into the gardening metaphor likening the internet we know today as streams. In his essay he says

"In the stream metaphor you don’t experience the Stream by walking around it and looking at it, or following it to its end. You jump in and let it flow past. You feel the force of it hit you as things float by.

It’s not that you are passive in the Stream. You can be active. But your actions in there — your blog posts, @ mentions, forum comments — exist in a context that is collapsed down to a simple timeline of events that together form a narrative."

In contrast, imagine a digital garden. You plant ideas as seeds, and continue to water them by adding to it and connecting them all together. As Mike writes, It's a tool to think with, not a tool to publish with.
### How digital gardening helps your creativity

Okay, now you know what digital gardening is, and it's origin story. But why should you start one and how does it help your creativity?

1. First, it can be as a home for all things your inspired by: a book you really liked and a movie that you loved, an article you read. It can be images, a quote, a screenshot. All of these are possible items you can include in your digital garden. 
   
   And you can take it one step further. Anna Howard in her video essay about digital gardens talks about using a digital garden to consume media thoughtfully. After you watch said movie, write what your takeaways were, what did you notice? Did you have any unanswered questions? Which leads me to
2. You become an active learner. 
   Since digital gardening, I've been actually taking more notes. After I watch a How To video, I take notes. A library book, notes. This helps the information sink in, and gives them staying power. In the past, after I watch anything, I would just watch the next thing. And whatever I learned simply dissolved with time. 
3. You get to know you better
   In Keep Going by Austin Kleon, he writes about how to stay creative. One of his points is to Pay attention to what you pay attention to. What are the things that you're inspired by? That bring you joy? Then he recommends collecting these things whether by writing, or by drawings and look for patterns.  I think digital gardening does a great job of mapping out your interests in a way that makes it easier to spot patterns. It's even easier when you use the tag feature. More on that later.
   
   And through these patterns, it reveals something about you to you. What concept keeps coming up? Seeing your interests as a whole can spark an idea.
4. It's a way to be online without being ONLINE. There's less pressure and there's no algorithm. A digital garden is not like a blog or instagram - you don't have to publish an article or even finished thoughts. You don't even have to publish it! Digital gardening is not focused on an outcome. It's driven by curiosity, and it's a journey.

### How to use it as a creative

You know the pros, now how can *you* use it.Something I struggled with, and why it took me so long to adopt digital gardening was that I didn't find many public digital gardens that I could relate to.

Because of it's non-linear format and interconnecting structure, the people who took to digital gardening best were people who could code, developers, etc. I considered myself a coder until I had to open a github account and look up what clone a repository meant in order to publish my digital garden. More on that later.

Even at the time of this video, I haven't found many public digital gardens that feature makers, creatives. Even though I think they would benefit so much from a digital garden. I did find a few and I'll put my favorites down below.

It wasn't until I came across Carolyn Yoo's digital garden that it finally clicked with me. Carolyn is an artist and writer and I love her substack. Her digital garden is public and it's a personal wiki. A list of how-tos. A collection of concepts. References. Honestly it was seeing the list of how-tos that inspired me to start my own. I consume so many how-to videos/articles - it's so nice to compile them in a space I can visit and navigate easily.

If you're an artist, you can use a digital garden to collect design inspiration from books and online. You can document your creative process, and if you're feeling up for it, show it to potential employers in an interview. What I've been doing, is going through my different art books and taking photos of things that inspire me and putting them in my digital garden - so it's all in one place where I can see.

If you're a writer, you can collect quotes, and write stories. Actually many writers use Obsidian to create their literary worlds. 

If you're a maker, you can collect how-to tutorials that you find on YouTube or blogs on your craft or on how to own a business. 

It's also a great tool to write a project diary. If you're working on a specific project like I am for this signet idea I have, you can write down your findings, what worked, what didn't, what adjustments you made. I find collecting these help your future self as well in case you want to ever reiterate on that project. 

You don't have to think, what did I buy for that project? Or for me, where did I get this casted? Project diaries are very useful.
### How to Start Your Own
If you're a coder - you can build your own! If not, there's a couple tools you can use: Obsidian, Notion are just two of them. There's also Are.na and Roam Garden. In this video, I'll just talk about the first two, since they are the most popular with digital gardening.

Whichever you choose largely depends on your needs. I like Obsidian because of it's simplicity. It's a free app that's mostly text-heavy, but they do have this feature called canvas where you can map out photos. I use that to collect inspo pics.

If you want to publish your Obsidian digital garden, it is $8 a month. If you want to publish online for free like I have you can use Quartz, but you'll need a little github experience, or at the very least an account . Let me know if you want a video on that. My favorite little feature is the brain map. It's just sooo satisfying to see it fill up the more you write and add to your digital garden.

Notion is great too! There are digital gardening templates that you can start with so you don't have to start completely from scratch. What I've personally found is sometimes Notion offers me too much personalization and freedom, that I get caught up in making it look good rather than actually writing. But again! It's all a preference. Notion doesn't require coding knowledge. I'll put additional helpful links below if you want to start your own.

### Conclusion

My current hyperfixation as of late? Digital gardening.

Since deleting Instagram and Tiktok from my phone, I've been struggling on how to show up on the internet. It's ironic because I've always been able to find a community way before Instagram and Facebook entered the picture. I've been a part of forums, built my own website, started Neopets guilds. I would make flashing GIFs (called blinkies) and PSDs (cropped out image of celebrities) for visitors to my website. 

Back then, the internet felt *fun* with boundless opportunities to be creative. 

I first heard about Digital Gardening back in 2020. And at the time, it didn't make sense. It's not a blog? It's not a Pinterest board? What is it? I struggled to apply it to my own life and needs. 

I then watched an incredible video essay by Anna Howard about digital gardening. And I was intrigued! She did a great job articulating not only what a digital garden is, but how it could benefit your creative life.

I downloaded Obsidian, even wrote a few notes here and there. But I had this pervasive feeling that I wasn't using it to its full potential and that it wasnt actually solving any problem I had.

Then enter [Carolyn Yoo's substack on Digital Gardening](https://cyoo.substack.com/p/intro-to-digital-gardening-a-quietly). Seeing her actual digital garden really made me see the potential of having one as a maker. A little wikipedia database personalized to your hyperfixations, obssessions, or deep dives. 

I've only been using a digital garden for a week and already I see my relationship with the internet transforming into something positive. For example, I'm collecting photos from the Public Domain and Internet Archive for project ideas. I'm re-listening to some of my favorite YouTube essays so I can log my favorite takeaways.

I am SO excited to grow this digital garden. 

So if you consider yourself a creative and want to make a digital garden, read on.
