### What is digital gardening?
Digital gardening is like a personal wiki. A second-brain. A common-place journal. It’s a place that you can create online that encourages continuous learning, exploration, and growth, much like tending to a physical garden
"The gardening approach is used, for example, to conceptualize online spaces where seeds (or thoughts) are nurtured and grown, crossfertilization of formerly separate entries allow for new reflections,and gardening processes require constant care to flourish" [[Digital gardening with a forest atlas]]

It's not considered a blog, because it's non-chronological. You're encouraged to build upon topics, as if you're growing a garden. 

It's not a mood board, because the goal is to connect the dots. Connect two different ideas or sources, and come up with a uniquely you idea. Learning in public

### How did it start?
The first recorded mention of the term Digital Gardening is Mark Bernstein's 1998 essay Hypertext Gardens. But how we use them today is more similar to [blanks] essay.

### How digital gardening helps your creativity

Digital gardening is not focused on an outcome. It's driven by curiosity, and it's a journey.

1. It serves as a home for all things your inspired by: a book you really liked and a movie that you loved, an article you read. It can be images, a quote, a screenshot. All of these are possible items you can include in your digital garden
2. Less pressure. A digital garden is not like a blog or instagram - you don't have to publish an article or even finished thoughts. Technically, you don't even have to share it! 
3. You notice patterns
   In Keep Going by Austin Kleon, he writes about how to stay creative. One of his points is to Pay attention to what you pay attention to. What are the things that you're inspired by? That bring you joy? Then he recommends collecting these things whether by writing, or by drawings and look for patterns. And through these patterns, it reveals something about you to you. I think digital gardening does a great job of mapping out your interests in a way that makes it easier to spot patterns.
   
   It's even easier when you use the tag feature. For example, say I make a note about Essay writing last year, and then come across a really informative substack about how write essays today. I can add what I've learned into that, and link them together with tags.
   
   It's also malleable. Unlike Pinterest, you can connect a quote to a photo to an article to an essay.  You're not just restricted by photos.
1. . You can connect two thoughts to create new ideas
   Related to the previous thought about patterns, Because of the bilateral links and tagging system, you can literally see the connection between two ideas. 
2. It's a way to be on the internet that's not beholden to an algorithm. God knows there's not many spaces like that anymore. There's no ads. No feed. Just a good old-school internet page.
3. You become an active learner
   Since digital gardening, I've been actually taking notes from things that I consume. Like a YouTube video on how to make essays. Or on a book. This helps the information sink in, and give them staying power. It also helps I can always refer back to them.

### How to use it as a creative

Something I struggled with, and why it took me so long to adopt digital gardening was that I didn't find many digital gardens that I could relate to.

Because of it's non-linear format and "wildness", the people who took to digital gardening best were people who could code, developers, etc. I considered myself a coder until I had to open a github account and look up what clone a repository meant in order to publish my digital garden. More on that later.

Even at the time of this video, I haven't found many public digital gardens that feature makers, creatives. Even though I think they would benefit so much from a digital garden. I'll put my favorites down below.

It wasn't until I came across Carolyn Yoo's digital garden that it finally clicked with me. Carolyn is an artist and writer. Her digital garden is public and it's a personal wiki. A list of how-tos. A few sources. It's not formatted as a polished blog. It honestly feels like peering into what I would imagine would be her purse notebook, that would contain book recommendations, places to go, etc. 

For me, I organize my digital garden with the following:
- How tos
- Projects
- References 
- Notes.

All of these are public, except Notes which are where I connect all of the different dots. I think those feel way more personal and honestly too much of a rough draft for me to comfortably show. I feel like these are self explanatory, but I'll go open a few. 


So there's different ways to use digital gardening as a creative: Anna Howard in her amazing video about Digital Gardening encourages writing down your thoughts after watching a film or reading an article, or even a song that sparked something in you. 

#### Examples
If you're an artist, you can use a digital garden to collect design inspiration from books and online. You can document your creative process, and if you're feeling up for it, show it to potential employers in an interview. What I've been doing, is going through my different art books and taking photos of things that inspire me and putting them in my digital garden - so it's all in one place where I can see. 

If you're a writer, you can collect quotes, and write stories. Actually many writers use Obsidian to create their literary worlds. 

If you're a maker, you can collect how-to tutorials that you find on YouTube or blogs on your craft or on how to own a business. It's also a great tool to write a project diary. If you're working on a specific project like I am for this signet idea I have, you can write down your findings, what worked, what didn't, what adjustments you made. I find collecting these help your future self as well in case you want to ever reiterate on that project. 

You don't have to think, what did I buy for that project? Or for me, where did I get this casted? Project diaries are very useful.

### How to Start Your Own
There's a couple tools you can use: Obsidian, Notion are just two of them. I mention these because they are free and can host images and text. Versus other ones like are.na, which I find to be more similar to pinterest in different ways. 

I use Obsidian, mostly because I love the mind map part of it. Notion is great too. Whichever you choose largely depends on your needs. I like Obsidian because of it's simplicity. But if you want to publish online for free like I have, you have to have a little github experience. Let me know if you want a video on that.

Notion is great too! There are digital gardening templates that are great, I'll list some down below. What I've personally found is sometimes Notion offers me too much personalization and freedom, that I get caught up in making it look good rather than actually writing. But again! It's all a preference. Notion doesn't require coding knowledge. If you want to create your own on Notion, Maggie Appleton makes a [great tutorial](https://maggieappleton.com/nontechnical-gardening) and goes over pros and cons in better detail.
### My experience

Since digital gardening, I'm actually motivated to collect sources, to write my thoughts on them. It's made me not only consume content thoughtfully, but actually seek out and research. I want a loooong list of How tos for future me to visit. I want to see the patterns develop in the little brain map. But those are things that come with time and tending to, just like an actual garden.

Right now, I'm working a flipping wax signet and it's been so cool to use the Obisidan canvas feature to paste inspo pics and map them all together. I've also been journaling through my creative process with this project which you can read in my digital garden. 
### Conclusion



My current hyperfixation as of late? Digital gardening.

Since deleting Instagram and Tiktok from my phone, I've been struggling on how to show up on the internet. It's ironic because I've always been able to find a community way before Instagram and Facebook entered the picture. I've been a part of forums, built my own website, started Neopets guilds. I would make flashing GIFs (called blinkies) and PSDs (cropped out image of celebrities) for visitors to my website. 

Back then, the internet felt *fun* with boundless opportunities to be creative. 

I first heard about Digital Gardening back in 2020. And at the time, it didn't make sense. It's not a blog? It's not a Pinterest board? What is it? I struggled to apply it to my own life and needs. 

I then watched an incredible video essay by Anna Howard about digital gardening. And I was intrigued! She did a great job articulating not only what a digital garden is, but how it could benefit your creative life.

I downloaded Obsidian, even wrote a few notes here and there. But I had this pervasive feeling that I wasn't using it to its full potential and that it wasnt actually solving any problem I had.

Then enter [Carolyn Yoo's substack on Digital Gardening](https://cyoo.substack.com/p/intro-to-digital-gardening-a-quietly). Seeing her actual digital garden really made me see the potential of having one as a maker. A little wikipedia database personalized to your hyperfixations, obssessions, or deep dives. 

I've only been using a digital garden for a week and already I see my relationship with the internet transforming into something positive. For example, I'm collecting photos from the Public Domain and Internet Archive for project ideas. I'm re-listening to some of my favorite YouTube essays so I can log my favorite takeaways.

I am SO excited to grow this digital garden. 

So if you consider yourself a creative and want to make a digital garden, read on.

For context, I grew up with the internet - I've had a xanga, blogspots, my own website, multiple neopets accounts, active on forums since I was maybe 9 years old. It's almost come to the point where not posting something feels unnatural to me. And this is not to say, I miss the views/likes/comments. I don't mind creating something and not sharing it. To be honest, I haven't told any of my friends I have this video channel. 

All this to say, sharing is part of the creative process for me. So how do I do that without the usual channels: instagram, tiktok, 

For me, I've never considered myself as an academic. That personal curriculum trend that happened in 2025? Sounded like unpaid work to me. But the thing is, I love learning. But not like read a big text book kid of learning, at least not yet. I gravitate towards a substack, or a youtube essay, or a book. I go into deep rabbit holes for weeks. And what do I show for it? Honestly, until now nothing. That information evaporates in my head as soon as the next hyperfixation comes along. Rinse and repeat.

So, I've been feeling creatively aimless lately. I have sooo many ideas and they would putter out once a big (or even small!) life event would come around. There is something to acting out on a creative impulse immediately. But what about the random hyperfixations and rabbit-holes?  The ideas that spark curiosity or joy within you, but you don't know what to do with just yet?

Sure there is Pinterest. But at least for me images get forgotten or lost until you actually open up the folder later. I can't tell you how many times Pinterest tells me I've already pinned an idea. Like, what do you mean I've already seen this before?
