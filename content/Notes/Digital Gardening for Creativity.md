So, I've been feeling creatively aimless lately. I have sooo many ideas and they would putter out once a big (or even small!) life event would come around. There is something to acting out on a creative impulse immediately. But what about the random hyperfixations and rabbit-holes? And I think I've found a solution: digital gardening.
### What is digital gardening?
Digital gardening is like a personal wiki. A second-brain. A common-place journal.

"The gardening approach is used, for example, to conceptualize online spaces where seeds (or thoughts) are nurtured and grown, crossfertilization of formerly separate entries allow for new reflections,and gardening processes require constant care to flourish" [[Digital gardening with a forest atlas]]

It's not considered a blog, because it's non-chronological. You're encouraged to build upon topics, as if you're growing a garden. 

It's not a mood board, because the goal is to connect the dots. Connect two different ideas or sources, and come up with a uniquely you idea. 

### How did it start?
The first recorded mention of the term Digital Gardening is Mark Bernstein's 1998 essay Hypertext Gardens.

### How digital gardening helps your creativity

Digital gardening is not focused on an outcome. It's driven by curiosity, and it's a journey.

2. It serves as a home for all things your inspired by: a book you really liked and a movie that you loved, an article you read. It can be images, a quote, a screenshot. All of these are possible items you can include in your digital garden
3. Less pressure. A digital garden is not like a blog or instagram - you don't have to publish an article or even finished thoughts. Technically, you don't even have to share it! 
4. You notice patterns
   In Keep Going by Austin Kleon, he writes about how to stay creative. One of his points is to Pay attention to what you pay attention to. What are the things that you're inspired by? That bring you joy? Then he recommends collecting these things whether by writing, or by drawings and look for patterns. I think digital gardening does a great job of mapping out your interests in a way that makes it easier to spot patterns.
   
   It's even easier when you use the tag feaature. For example, say I make a note about Essay writing last year, and then come across a really informative substack about how write essay today. I can add what I've learned into that, and link them together with tags.
   
   It's also malleable. Unlike Pinterest, you can connect a quote to a photo to an article to an essay. 
2. It's a way to be on the internet that's not beholden to an algorithm. God knows there's not many spaces like that anymore. 

### How to use it as a creative

Something I struggled with, and why it took me so long to adopt digital gardening was that I didn't find many digital gardens that I could relate to.

Because of it's non-linear format and "wildness", the people who took to digital gardening best were people who could code, developers, etc. I considered myself a coder until I had to open a github account and look up what clone a repository meant in order to publish my digital garden. More on that later.

Even at the time of this video, I haven't found many public digital gardens that feature makers, creatives. Even though I think they would benefit so much from a digital garden.  

It wasn't until I came across Carolyn Yoo's digital garden that it finally clicked with me. Carolyn is an artist and writer. Her digital garden is public and it's a personal wiki. A list of how-tos. A few sources. It's not formatted as a polished blog. It honestly feels like peering into her purse notebook, that would contain book recommendations, places to go, etc. 

For me, I organize my digital garden with the following:
- How tos
- Projects
- References 
- Notes

I feel like these are self explanatory, but I'll go open a few. 

Anna Howard in her amazing video about Digital Gardening encourages writing down your thoughts after watching a film or reading an article that sparked something in you. It doesn't have to be polished, in fact, by definition it shouldn't be. 
### How to Start Your Own
There's a couple tools you can use: Obsidian, Notion are just two of them.

I use Obsidian, mostly because I love the mind map part of it. Notion is great too.  Whichever you choose largely depends on your needs. I like Obsidian because of it's simplicity. Sometimes Notion offers me too much personalization and freedom, that I get caught up in making it look good rather than actually writing. But again! It's all a preference.
### My experience

Since digital gardening, I'm actually motivated to collect sources, to write my thoughts on them. It's made me not only consume content thoughtfully, but actually seek out content. I want a loooong list of How tos to visit. I want to see the patterns develop in the little brain map. 

Right now, I'm working a flipping wax signet and it's been so cool to use the Obisidan canvas feature to paste inspo pics and map them all together. 
### Conclusion

My current hyperfixation as of late? Digital gardening.

Since deleting Instagram and Tiktok from my phone, I've been struggling on how to show up on the internet. It's ironic because I've always been able to find a community way before Instagram and Facebook entered the picture. I've been a part of forums, built my own website, started Neopets guilds. I would make flashing GIFs (called blinkies) and PSDs (cropped out image of celebrities) for visitors to my website. 

Back then, the internet felt *fun* with boundless opportunities to be creative. 

I first heard about Digital Gardening back in 2020. And at the time, it didn't make sense. It's not a blog? It's not a Pinterest board? What is it? I struggled to apply it to my own life and needs. 

I then watched an incredible video essay by Anna Howard about digital gardening. And I was intrigued! She did a great job articulating not only what a digital garden is, but how it could benefit your creative life.

I downloaded Obsidian, even wrote a few notes here and there. But I had this pervasive feeling that I wasn't using it to its full potential and that it wasnt actually solving any problem I had.

Then enter [Carolyn Yoo's substack on Digital Gardening](https://cyoo.substack.com/p/intro-to-digital-gardening-a-quietly). Seeing her actual digital garden really made me see the potential of having one as a maker. A little wikipedia database personalized to your hyperfixations, obssessions, or deep dives. 

I've only been using a digital garden for a week and already I see my relationship with the internet transforming into something positive. For example, I'm collecting photos from the Public Domain and Internet Archive for project ideas. I'm re-listening to some of my favorite YouTube essays so I can log my favorite takeaways.

I am SO excited to grow this digital garden. 

So if you consider yourself a creative and want to make a digital garden, read on.
